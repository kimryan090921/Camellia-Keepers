From Silence to Archive: The Jeju 4.3 Incident
and the Study of Collective Memory
Introduction
History is not simply a record of what happened. It is also shaped by what societies choose to remember,
preserve, and sometimes forget. The Jeju 4.3 Incident provides a powerful example of this relationship between
history and memory. Beginning in 1948, political conflict on Jeju developed into years of violence in which large
numbers of civilians were killed. Yet the legacy of Jeju 4.3 was shaped not only by the violence itself, but also by
decades in which victims and their families struggled to speak openly about their experiences. For this reason,
Jeju 4.3 can be studied through the academic field of memory studies, particularly the concept of collective
memory. This field examines how groups construct understandings of the past through testimony, photographs,
documents, memorials, museums, and public ceremonies. Jeju 4.3 demonstrates how preserving evidence can
transform suppressed private memories into publicly recognised history.
Historical Context
The origins of Jeju 4.3 can be traced to the unstable political situation following Korea's liberation from Japanese
colonial rule in 1945. Political disagreements between left-wing and right-wing groups intensified in the emerging
Cold War. On Jeju, resistance to separate elections in southern Korea contributed to growing tension, and an
armed uprising began on 3 April 1948. Counterinsurgency operations followed, and the conflict affected
communities across the island. The violence continued in different forms for several years.
The destruction did not end when the violence decreased. For decades, discussion of Jeju 4.3 remained
politically sensitive. Survivors and relatives of victims could face suspicion because of alleged associations with
communism. Many families therefore avoided discussing their experiences. This silence shows why historical
memory is not created only through individual recollection: political and social conditions can determine which
memories enter public history and which remain hidden.

Jeju 4.3 and Collective Memory
Collective memory refers to the way understandings of the past are produced and maintained within social
groups. Individuals remember personally, but societies preserve memories through shared narratives,
monuments, photographs, archives, education, and rituals. Jeju 4.3 illustrates what can happen when this
process is interrupted. Survivors possessed memories of destroyed villages, lost relatives, arrests,
displacement, and fear, but these experiences were often unable to become part of the country's official
historical narrative.
Silence therefore became historically important in itself. If testimony is not recorded and documents are not
preserved, later generations may struggle to reconstruct an event. Memory studies consequently asks not only,
What happened? but also, How was what happened remembered? Historical documents can provide evidence
about institutions and events, while oral testimony can preserve individual experiences that official documents
[[page 2]]
fail to record. Photographs offer another form of evidence by preserving people, places, and communities that
might otherwise disappear from public memory.
Archives as Historical Evidence
This connection between memory and evidence can be seen clearly in the development of the Jeju 4.3
Archives. Archives are not simply storage spaces. In archival studies, decisions about what is collected,
categorised, preserved, digitised, and displayed influence how future generations understand history.
Collections relating to Jeju 4.3 include official records, photographs, testimony, publications, and other
documentary materials. Bringing these sources together allows researchers to compare institutional records
with personal experiences and reconstruct aspects of the past that were excluded from public discussion.
The international significance of this work increased in 2025 when UNESCO added Revealing Truth: Jeju 4.3
Archives to the Memory of the World International Register. UNESCO describes the collection as 14,673
documents related to Jeju 4.3 and emphasises their role in preserving suppressed memories, restoring the
honour of victims, and documenting local efforts toward reconciliation. The recognition is important because
material connected to a once-silenced event is now treated as documentary heritage with international
significance.

Memory, Place and Memorialisation
Collective memory is also created through physical space. Memorial sites connected to Jeju 4.3 allow visitors to
encounter names, photographs, documents, exhibitions, and landscapes associated with the event. This
creates a different relationship with history from reading dates and statistics alone. A death toll communicates
scale, but a victim's name or photograph connects that scale with an individual life. Memorialisation therefore
shapes how people who did not experience an event directly can understand and emotionally engage with it.

Transitional Justice and Public Recognition
Jeju 4.3 can also be connected to the field of transitional justice, which examines how societies respond to
past political violence and human-rights violations through truth investigations, official acknowledgement,
memorialisation, legal measures, and reparative policies. South Korea's democratisation created greater
opportunities for public investigation of Jeju 4.3. The Special Act on the Jeju 4.3 Incident was passed in 2000,
an official investigation followed, and President Roh Moo-hyun issued a state apology in 2003. These
developments show memory moving from the private sphere into public institutions.
Archives are especially important in this process because acknowledgement depends on evidence.
Testimonies, government documents, photographs, and other records allow investigators to test historical
claims and reconstruct events more systematically. Memory and justice are therefore closely connected: without
preserved evidence, official investigation and recognition become far more difficult.
Personal Reflection
Studying Jeju 4.3 through collective memory changes the meaning of an archive. An archive can appear to be
only a collection of old documents, but in this case it has a wider social function. A testimony preserves a voice.
A photograph preserves a person or place. A government document can reveal how institutions understood and
responded to an event. When these sources are brought together, later generations can reconstruct
experiences that might otherwise disappear.
This is particularly meaningful in Jeju because many people lived for decades with memories that were difficult
to discuss publicly. Preserving those memories therefore represents more than academic research. It marks a
transition from silence toward recognition. It also shows that the study of history is not only about discovering
facts; it is about understanding how evidence survives, who is allowed to speak, and how societies decide what
deserves to be remembered.
Conclusion
[[page 4]]
The Jeju 4.3 Incident demonstrates that the past does not automatically become history. History depends on
people preserving evidence, recording testimony, challenging incomplete narratives, and transmitting
knowledge to future generations. Through memory studies, Jeju 4.3 can therefore be understood not only as a
historical episode but as a continuing process of remembering. Photographs, oral testimony, archives,
museums, and memorials have transformed memories that were once largely private into public historical
evidence.
The inclusion of the Jeju 4.3 Archives in UNESCO's Memory of the World Register represents an important
stage in this process. Archives cannot change what happened, but they can influence whether the experiences
of those who lived through history are forgotten, distorted, or understood. Jeju 4.3 therefore shows how
collective memory, archival studies, and transitional justice can work together to explain why preserving the past
matters in the present.
