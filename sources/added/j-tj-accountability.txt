Transitional Justice and the Jeju 4.3 Incident: Truth, Reparations, and the Limits of
Accountability
Introduction
The Jeju 4.3 Incident, which began on April 3, 1948, and lasted until September 1954, left an
estimated 25,000 to 30,000 people dead, roughly one in ten residents of Jeju Island. For
more than half a century, however, the dead were not mourned publicly. They were officially
remembered, if at all, as rebels. Today, Jeju 4.3 is widely studied as a case of transitional
justice, the field concerned with how societies confront large-scale past abuses through
truth-seeking, prosecution, reparations, and institutional reform. The case is instructive
because South Korea did not address Jeju 4.3 in the immediate aftermath of violence or
regime change, but decades later, through a long and uneven process driven largely by
survivors and civil society. It shows both what transitional justice can achieve and where it
falls short.
Historical Context
After liberation from Japanese rule in 1945, Korea was divided under U.S. and Soviet
occupation, and political polarization deepened. On Jeju, opposition to the separate
elections planned for the southern half of the peninsula escalated into an armed uprising in
1948. The response from the South Korean government, with the backing of U.S. military
authorities, was a brutal counterinsurgency campaign. Mountain villages were burned, and
civilians were frequently classified as communist sympathizers without evidence.
The violence was followed by decades of enforced silence. Under successive
anti-communist authoritarian governments, the state portrayed the event as a communist
riot. Families of victims faced surveillance and social discrimination, and public mourning
was effectively impossible. This history matters for transitional justice because it shows that
the harm of Jeju 4.3 was not limited to the killings themselves. It included the long denial of
truth, dignity, and voice to those who survived.
Truth-Seeking and the 2000 Special Act
Transitional justice scholars often describe truth-seeking as the foundation of any later
reform. Priscilla Hayner argues that official truth commissions can break the cycle of denial
by creating an authoritative account of what happened. After South Korea's democratization
in 1987, local activists, journalists, bereaved families, and artists began pressing for
investigation. Their efforts led to the Special Act on the Jeju 4.3 Incident in 2000, which
established a government committee to investigate the events and restore the honor of
victims.
The committee's 2003 report concluded that the majority of the killings were committed by
state security forces
[[page 2]]
and that many victims were civilians, including children and the elderly. Later that year,
President Roh Moo-hyun offered an official apology on behalf of the state. Scholars such as
Hun Joon Kim have emphasized that this achievement was remarkable precisely because it
came from the bottom up. The movement succeeded by building coalitions across Jeju
society and eventually by gaining support from national political leaders. Its success was not
inevitable but the result of sustained civic mobilization.
Reparations and Memory
The United Nations framework for transitional justice, later articulated by Special Rapporteur
Pablo de Greiff, identifies four pillars: truth, justice, reparations, and guarantees of
non-recurrence. Jeju has made progress on several of these fronts. The Jeju 4.3 Peace
Park, opened in 2008, gives the dead a permanent and public place of remembrance. In
2014, the government designated April 3 as a national day of commemoration. In recent
years, courts have acquitted victims in retrials of earlier military tribunal convictions, and
legislation passed in 2021 created a system of monetary compensation for victims and
bereaved families.
These measures matter because reparations are not merely financial. They signal that the
state recognizes victims as rights-holders rather than as enemies. Symbolic reparations
such as memorials and apologies restore social standing, while material reparations
acknowledge concrete losses. Together they challenge the long legacy of stigma that once
attached to survivors and their descendants.

Aftermath and Remaining Gaps
Despite these achievements, Jeju also illustrates the limits of transitional justice. The pillar of
justice, understood as accountability for perpetrators, remains largely unfulfilled. Because so
much time has passed, most of those directly responsible have died, and the original
investigation was designed to establish facts and restore honor rather than to prosecute.
[[page 4]]
Critics also note that questions about the role of the U.S. military government have not been
fully answered, and survivors have continued to call for a clearer acknowledgment from
Washington. Some conservative groups in South Korea have also continued to contest the
event's official interpretation, showing that historical memory remains politically contested
rather than settled.
Transitional justice theorist Ruti Teitel has argued that such processes are inherently shaped
by their political circumstances, meaning that justice is often partial and negotiated rather
than complete. Jeju supports this view. Its progress was real, but it depended heavily on who
held political power at a given time, and it remains vulnerable to changes in political climate.
Personal Reflection
What I find most striking about the Jeju case is how much it depended on ordinary people.
International models of transitional justice often focus on tribunals, commissions, and legal
frameworks, but in Jeju the process began with families who refused to forget. Learning
about this, I realized that justice is not only something that institutions deliver to society; it is
also something that society demands from institutions, sometimes over decades.
It also changes how I think about the word "transition." We often imagine a clear moment
when a society moves from violence to peace. In Jeju, the transition has been slow, partial,
and still unfinished. This suggests that justice should be understood less as a single event
and more as a continuing responsibility, one that each generation must choose to uphold.
Conclusion
The Jeju 4.3 Incident shows how transitional justice can function even long after the violence
has ended. Through truth-seeking, apology, memorialization, and reparations, South Korea
has moved from official denial toward public acknowledgment. At the same time, gaps in
accountability, international responsibility, and political consensus show that the process
remains incomplete.
Studying Jeju through this lens helps clarify what justice after mass violence can realistically
accomplish. It cannot undo the deaths, but it can restore dignity, correct the historical record,
and build a society less willing to repeat the silence that once surrounded the victims. As the
case demonstrates, the work of justice does not end with an apology. It continues in
remembrance, education, and the willingness to keep asking difficult questions.
