Long-Term Economic Scarring from Mass Violence:
The Legacy of the Jeju 4.3 Incident
The trajectory of a society’s economic development is often explained through measurable factors
such as policy decisions, natural resources, technological progress, and participation in global trade.
However, beneath these visible indicators lies a deeper and often overlooked force: the enduring
damage caused by mass violence. Long-term economic scarring refers to the persistent and structural
reduction in a society’s productive capacity following periods of intense conflict. Unlike cyclical
economic downturns, which may recover over time, the effects of mass violence can permanently
alter a region’s developmental path. This occurs through the destruction of human capital, the collapse
of economic and social institutions, and the erosion of trust that underpins all economic interaction.
The Jeju 4.3 Incident provides a powerful and deeply illustrative case study of this phenomenon.
Between 1948 and 1954, the island of Jeju experienced a period of violent state suppression that
resulted in the deaths of approximately 30,000 people—around 10% of its population—and the
destruction of more than 70% of its villages. While the immediate human cost was devastating, the
long-term economic consequences were equally profound. These effects became embedded in Jeju’s
social and economic fabric, delaying its development for decades and widening the gap between the
island and mainland South Korea.
I. The Destruction of Human Capital
At its core, an economy is sustained by human capital—the collective skills, knowledge, health, and
labor capacity of its population. Mass violence represents one of the most severe forms of human
capital destruction, as it not only eliminates individuals but also disrupts the systems through which
knowledge and skills are passed on.
During the Jeju 4.3 Incident, violence disproportionately targeted working-age adults, particularly
men who formed the backbone of the island’s agricultural and fishing economy. This led to an
immediate “labor vacuum,” sharply reducing productive capacity. Farms were left untended, fisheries
underutilized, and local industries struggled to function even at a basic level.

As illustrated in Figure 1, the demographic impact was heavily concentrated among those in their
prime working years. Individuals aged 10 to 39 accounted for over 66% of the total victims,
intensifying the labor vacuum and stripping the island not only of its current workforce but also of its
future economic leaders.
However, the damage extended far beyond this immediate labor shortage. The incident also triggered
a deeper and more enduring form of economic disruption: intergenerational knowledge loss. In 1948,
Jeju’s economy was largely subsistence-based, relying on agricultural techniques adapted to its
volcanic soil and unique climate. These methods were rarely written down; instead, they were
transmitted through oral tradition and daily practice. The deaths of elders, village leaders, and skilled
workers severed this chain of knowledge. As a result, survivors were left without the expertise and
mentorship needed to sustain productivity, leading to long-term declines in agricultural efficiency.
The demographic consequences further compounded these challenges. The large-scale loss of
working-age individuals skewed the population toward dependents—the elderly and orphaned
children—creating a high dependency ratio. This forced the remaining workforce to focus on
immediate survival rather than long-term investment in education, innovation, or capital development.
Over time, this imbalance created a self-reinforcing cycle of low productivity and limited economic
mobility, effectively stalling economic growth for decades.
II. Physical Destruction and the "Scorched Earth" Policy
The economic scarring of Jeju was not only demographic but also physical. The government’s
“scorched earth” policy systematically dismantled the island’s infrastructure, leaving lasting damage
that extended far beyond the years of active conflict.
More than 300 villages were burned, erasing entire communities and the local economic networks that
sustained them. These villages were not merely residential areas but centers of production, trade, and
social organization. Their destruction disrupted the exchange of goods and services, effectively
collapsing local markets and isolating surviving populations.
Agriculture, the foundation of Jeju’s pre-incident economy, was particularly hard hit. Livestock were
slaughtered or lost during the violence, irrigation systems fell into disrepair, and fields were
abandoned. Because agriculture depends heavily on continuity—seasonal cycles, soil management,
and accumulated experience—the interruption had long-lasting consequences. Even after the violence
subsided, the absence of labor, tools, and knowledge meant that recovery was slow and incomplete. In
the immediate aftermath, Jeju struggled to produce enough food to sustain its own population.
One of the most significant long-term consequences was the creation of “red zones”—areas
designated as unsafe or politically suspect, which remained abandoned for decades. These lands, once
productive, were effectively removed from economic use for over 50 years, representing a massive
loss of potential output.
This physical devastation contributed directly to regional inequality. While mainland South Korea
embarked on rapid industrialization during the 1960s—often referred to as the “Miracle on the Han
River”—Jeju remained focused on basic reconstruction. The island lacked the infrastructure, labor
force, and capital necessary to participate in this period of accelerated growth. As a result, a clear
development gap emerged, with Jeju becoming economically peripheral and structurally
disadvantaged compared to the mainland.

III. The Erosion of Social Trust and Institutional Neglect
While the destruction of human and physical capital is visible, the erosion of social trust is a more
subtle yet equally damaging consequence of mass violence. Economic activity depends fundamentally
on trust—the expectation that individuals can safely engage in trade, investment, and cooperation
without fear of exploitation or persecution.
In the aftermath of the Jeju 4.3 Incident, this trust was deeply undermined. The South Korean
government labeled Jeju as a “Red Island,” associating its მოსახლtion with political subversion.
Under the system of guilt by association (Yeon-jwa-je), the families and descendants of victims were
subjected to discrimination, surveillance, and exclusion from public sector employment.
This created what can be described as a “silence tax.” Individuals avoided drawing attention to
themselves, refrained from participating in collective activities, and hesitated to pursue
entrepreneurial ventures. The risk of being labeled politically suspect outweighed the potential
benefits of economic initiative. This pervasive fear suppressed innovation, reduced investment, and
weakened the social networks necessary for economic cooperation.
Institutional neglect further exacerbated these issues. Because the history of the incident was
politically suppressed, Jeju did not receive adequate government investment in infrastructure,
education, or public services. Schools were underfunded, transportation networks underdeveloped,
and healthcare systems insufficient. This lack of institutional support reinforced the island’s economic
stagnation, creating a cycle in which underdevelopment justified further neglect.
Over time, these factors combined to produce a deeply entrenched economic disadvantage. Unlike
regions that benefited from state-led industrial policies, Jeju remained marginalized, its development
constrained not only by material losses but also by the absence of trust and institutional support.
IV. The Turning Point: Reconciliation as Economic Stimulus
[[page 4]]
The case of Jeju demonstrates that economic recovery from mass violence is inseparable from social
and political reconciliation. It was only in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, following South
Korea’s democratization, that meaningful efforts were made to address the legacy of the 4.3 Incident.
A key turning point came with the official state apology in 2003, which acknowledged the
government’s role in the violence. This act of recognition played a crucial role in restoring dignity to
survivors and their descendants. By removing political stigmas and dismantling discriminatory
systems, it allowed Jeju residents to participate more fully in the national economy.
This shift also enabled a transformation in Jeju’s economic strategy. The island began to reposition
itself as a global tourism destination, leveraging its natural beauty and unique cultural heritage.
Importantly, sites associated with the 4.3 Incident were not erased but recontextualized. The Jeju 4.3
Peace Park stands as a symbol of this transformation, turning a place of historical trauma into a site of
education, remembrance, and cultural engagement.
Tourism brought significant investment, infrastructure development, and job creation, allowing Jeju to
integrate more fully into both national and global markets. However, this transformation was delayed
by decades due to the unresolved legacy of the incident. The case thus highlights how unresolved
historical trauma can act as a barrier to economic development, while reconciliation can serve as a
powerful catalyst for growth.
Conclusion
The Jeju 4.3 Incident illustrates that the consequences of mass violence extend far beyond the
immediate loss of life. The long-term economic scars—manifested in the destruction of human
capital, the dismantling of infrastructure, and the erosion of social trust—created a “lost half-century”
for Jeju Island. These effects were not temporary setbacks but structural changes that altered the
island’s developmental trajectory.
Recovery required more than physical reconstruction. It depended on restoring trust, addressing
historical injustices, and reintegrating marginalized communities into the broader economic system.
The eventual success of Jeju’s tourism industry demonstrates that healing and development are deeply
interconnected.
Ultimately, the case of Jeju offers a broader lesson for policymakers and societies worldwide. The
prevention of mass violence and the protection of human dignity are not only moral imperatives but
also essential foundations for sustainable economic development. Once inflicted, the scars of violence
are costly and enduring, shaping the fate of communities for generations.
