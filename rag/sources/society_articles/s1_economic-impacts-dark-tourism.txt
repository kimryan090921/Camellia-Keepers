The Economic Impacts of Jeju 4·3 Dark Tourism
While Jeju Island is internationally recognized for its natural attractions—including its
volcanic landscape, citrus production, and traditional haenyeo diving communities—it also
encompasses significant historical sites associated with the 1948–1954 Jeju 4·3 Incident. In
recent years, a growing cohort of tourists has traveled to Jeju specifically to visit these memorial
sites and gain historical understanding, a phenomenon commonly referred to as "4·3 dark
tourism." This form of heritage tourism has emerged as a meaningful component of Jeju's
economic landscape. This article examines the nature of the 4·3 Incident, defines 4·3 dark
tourism within the broader context of memorial tourism, and analyzes its quantifiable economic
impacts on local businesses, employment, and regional revenue generation.
Jeju 4·3 Incident
The Jeju 4·3 Incident, named for its commencement date of April 3, 1948, represents one
of the most significant episodes of civil unrest and military repression in twentieth-century
Korean history. During the politically unstable transition period between the conclusion of
Japanese colonial administration and the establishment of separate Korean states, the island
experienced a leftist-led uprising that was met with an extensive military and security force
response, involving both South Korean government forces and American military oversight. The
conflict persisted over a period of approximately six years. Contemporary historical estimates
indicate that approximately 30,000 inhabitants of Jeju—representing approximately one in nine
island residents—were killed during this period, with entire villages subject to systematic
destruction (Jeju Tourism Organization).
A distinctive feature of the 4·3 Incident in Korean historical memory is the prolonged
official denial and suppression that followed the immediate conflict period. For nearly five
decades, public discussion and acknowledgment of the incident remained politically constrained.
Survivors and bereaved family members experienced what historians have characterized as a
"double trauma," encompassing both the direct grief of loss and the institutional prohibition
against commemorative expression. The establishment of the 4·3 Special Law in 2000 initiated
the process of official state recognition. President Roh Moo-hyun became the first sitting
president to issue a formal governmental apology in 2003, followed by President Moon Jae-in's
public acknowledgment at the seventieth anniversary commemoration in 2018 (Jeju 4·3 Peace
Foundation).
4·3 Dark Tourism
Dark tourism, in its broadest conceptualization, encompasses travel to destinations
historically associated with death, suffering, or catastrophic events. Exemplary sites include
Auschwitz, the Cambodian genocide memorials, the 9/11 Memorial, and the Hiroshima Peace
Memorial. The term was formally introduced by Lennon and Foley in the mid-1990s and has
subsequently developed into a recognized and substantial sector of the global travel industry.
[[page 2]]
Market analysis indicates that the global dark tourism sector was valued at approximately USD
32.6 billion in 2024, with projections suggesting expansion to approximately USD 57 billion by
2032 (Credence Research).
Within the context of Jeju Island, 4·3 dark tourism assumes a distinctive form centered on
specific memorial and educational institutions. The primary institutional anchor is the Jeju 4·3
Peace Park, which commenced operations in 2008 following extended processes of legal
establishment and political negotiation. Occupying an area of approximately 400,000 square
meters in the Bonggae-dong district, the park comprises a memorial hall, a chronologically
organized museum documenting the historical events preceding and following the uprising, an
enshrinement facility containing memorial tablets for 14,000 identified victims, and a
"Tombstone Park for the Missing," which contains approximately 4,000 markers
commemorating individuals whose remains were not recovered (Jeju 4·3 Peace Foundation).
Complementary to the Peace Park are guided heritage tours operated by organizations
including Jeju Dark Tours, an organization founded in 2017 by human rights advocate Baek
Gayoon. These guided experiences direct visitors to specific historical sites within the broader
geographic region, including Bukchon village, where substantial casualties occurred in a
single-day operation; Darangshi Cave, where the remains of eleven villagers were discovered in
1992; Seodal Oreum, an execution site in the southwestern portion of the island; and Moksimul
Cave, which served as temporary refuge for civilian populations during military operations.
Tours are typically conducted in Korean with English-language interpretation available upon
request, operate on varying timescales from single-day to multi-night programs, and range in
cost from approximately ₩50,000 to ₩250,000 per participant. The primary distinction between
such heritage tourism and conventional recreational sightseeing lies in the prioritization of
historical education and interpretive comprehension as the primary objective of the visitor
experience.
Direct Economic Impact
Although the Jeju 4·3 Peace Park operates on a free admission basis, the facility has
demonstrated substantial annual visitor traffic. Estimated annual visitation has increased
progressively since the early 2010s, expanding from approximately 320,000 visitors in 2015 to
an estimated 720,000 in 2024, with the exception of a substantial decline during 2020–2021
corresponding to the COVID-19 pandemic. Significant increases in visitation occurred during
2018 and 2023, coinciding with the seventieth and seventy-fifth anniversary commemoration
periods (see Figure 1). The annual April 3 memorial ceremony attracts approximately 10,000
participants, and the memorial museum has administered educational programs for tens of
thousands of school-based groups annually (Jeju 4·3 Peace Foundation).
[[page 3]]
These temporal variations in visitation demonstrate correlation with periods of increased
media coverage and public commemorative activity. Both identified anniversary years coincided
with substantial media attention across television, print, and digital platforms, together with
associated increases in social media engagement. The documented relationship between
commemorative events and increased visitation numbers indicates the capacity of historical
memory initiatives to drive sustained cultural tourism.
However, the primary mechanism of economic impact operates independently of direct
admission revenue, which approximates zero. Rather, economic benefit derives from visitor
expenditures ancillary to the visitation experience. According to survey research conducted by
the Jeju Research Institute in 2023, visitors to 4·3 heritage sites maintained an average length of
stay of 2.7 days on Jeju Island and expended an estimated ₩285,000 per person across the
categories of accommodation, food service, transportation, and commodity purchases (Jeju
Research Institute). Extrapolating from the estimated annual visitation of 700,000 individuals
engaged with 4·3-related sites yields an aggregate estimate of approximately ₩199.5 billion
(approximately USD 145 million) in annual ancillary expenditure circulating through Jeju's
regional economy.
Indirect Impact on Local Businesses
The geographic distribution of 4·3 memorial sites across the island produce economic
consequences distinct from those associated with conventional beach-and-resort tourism models.
Rather than concentrating visitor expenditure in centralized resort areas such as Jungmun and
Aewol, dark tourism visitation generates distributed spending patterns across smaller
communities and peripheral economic zones. This dispersal pattern has resulted in measurable
[[page 4]]
benefits for food service establishments in proximity to the Peace Park, particularly within the
Bonggae-dong and Jocheon districts.
Research conducted by the Jeju Chamber of Commerce in 2022 examined the economic
performance of small-scale food service establishments operating within a three-kilometer radius
of the Peace Park facility. The findings indicated that such establishments experienced an
average revenue increase of 38 percent during peak heritage tourism seasons—specifically April
through June and September through November—compared to the same establishments'
performance a decade earlier (Jeju Chamber of Commerce). This increase reflects the integration
of local culinary traditions into guided tour itineraries, as authorized guides are incentivized to
direct visitor expenditure toward independently operated establishments rather than multinational
food service chains. Regional specialties including local pork barbecue, abalone-based soups,
and traditional Jeju noodle preparations feature prominently in tour programming.
The accommodation sector in Bukchon village provides a particularly illustrative
example of dark tourism's economic impact on historically economically marginal communities.
Bukchon village, the site of significant casualties during a single military operation, experienced
prolonged economic stagnation in the post-conflict period. Contemporary economic
revitalization of the area has been achieved in part through the development of heritage tourism
infrastructure, including restored traditional structures adapted for visitor accommodation. These
establishments maintain occupancy rates substantially elevated during the April commemorative
period, with accommodation pricing often doubling during this annually scheduled heritage
tourism peak.

Accommodation accounts for approximately 32 percent of visitor spending, food and beverage

of expenditure on guided tour services—approximately 12 percent of total visitor
spending—represents a substantively elevated proportion relative to mainstream tourism patterns
on Jeju, which typically generate guide service fees representing less than 4 percent of total
visitor expenditure. This differential reflects the interpretive and educational requirements
inherent in heritage tourism experiences, wherein the historical and contextual significance of
physical locations necessitates professional explanation.
Secondary economic benefits extend to the publishing and educational product sectors.
Independent bookstores operating on Jeju maintain inventory of translated editions of literary
works addressing the 4·3 Incident, including Hyun Ki-young's novel Sun-i Samch'on (Aunt
Suni), a foundational literary treatment that initiated significant public discourse regarding
previously suppressed historical events. Educational publishers document sustained demand
from school-based groups conducting curricular visits to the memorial complex. Small-scale
printing operations located in Jeju City maintain specialization in the production of tour
informational materials, exhibition catalogs, and commemorative publications supporting the
broader 4·3 heritage tourism infrastructure.
Employment and Longer-Term Effects
Quantitative assessment of employment generation attributable to 4·3 heritage tourism
presents methodological challenges due to the diffuse nature of economic integration across
multiple employment sectors. However, several baseline observations can be substantiated. The
Jeju 4·3 Peace Park operates with a permanent staff of approximately 80 individuals,
supplemented by seasonal docents and security personnel. Organizations including Jeju Dark
Tours and comparable heritage tourism operators maintain between two and ten full-time
personnel, together with supplementary freelance guide services. Extrapolating employment
effects across the broader tourism infrastructure supporting 4·3 visitation—encompassing
accommodations, food service, transportation, and retail sectors—conservative estimates place
the aggregate employment base at approximately 1,200 to 1,800 Jeju residents whose income
streams depend substantially on heritage tourism activity.
Contemporary research literature on dark tourism and memorial tourism identifies a
phenomenon designated as the "memorial dividend," characterized by sustained patterns of
visitor traffic demonstrating greater recession resistance relative to conventional leisure-based
tourism models (Sharpley and Stone). Auschwitz-Birkenau and the Hiroshima Peace Memorial,
both operating at the international scale, continue to demonstrate sustained visitation levels more
than seven decades following the historical events they commemorate. Comparable trajectories
appear probable for Jeju 4·3 heritage tourism, particularly given demographic trends including
[[page 6]]
increased international dissemination of Korean-language educational content and growing
cohorts of both Korean and international visitors pursuing historically informed travel.
Concurrent with these economic considerations exist substantive ethical and social
questions regarding the appropriate commemoration and interpretation of historical trauma.
Portions of the survivor and bereaved family communities have articulated concerns regarding
the potential commodification of collective tragedy, particularly when visitor conduct at
memorial sites manifests insufficient respect for the gravity of historical events. Optimal
management of these memorial spaces therefore necessitates integration of heritage tourism
development with protocols ensuring historical accuracy, visitor comportment standards, and
preservation of the dignity of affected communities—considerations extending beyond purely
economic assessment frameworks.
Conclusion
While 4·3 dark tourism does not function as a dominant driver of regional tourism
economics relative to conventional resort-based leisure tourism on Jeju, it operates as a
significant and strategically important economic sector within the island's broader tourism
portfolio. The phenomenon directs visitor expenditure into economically peripheral communities
and sectors historically underrepresented in mainstream tourism development, including
small-scale accommodation providers, independent food service establishments, and local
transportation services. The quantifiable economic impacts—estimated at approximately ₩199.5
billion in annual visitor expenditure—are complemented by qualitative contributions to cultural
preservation, historical education, and community economic revitalization.
The significance of 4·3 dark tourism extends beyond purely economic metrics. For
survivors and bereaved communities, the development of heritage tourism infrastructure
represents formal governmental and institutional recognition of suppressed historical events. The
sustained flow of both domestic and international visitors pursuing historical understanding
constitutes a mechanism through which collective memory operates and historical consciousness
develops. The economic sustainability of memorial sites and heritage tourism services is
fundamentally interconnected with the capacity to attract and serve visitors motivated by
educational and commemorative purposes. Sustainable management frameworks for such sites
must therefore integrate economic viability with historical accuracy, ethical representation, and
the dignity of affected populations—ensuring that heritage tourism functions as a vector for
historical understanding rather than commodification of tragedy.
