Labelling Theory and the Construction of “Communist”
Identities: The Case of the Jeju 4.3 Incident
The Jeju 4.3 Incident (1947–1954) remains one of the most controversial and traumatic
events in modern Korean history. While it began as a complex uprising rooted in political,
social, and economic grievances, it was subsequently framed by the South Korean state as
a “communist rebellion.” This labelling had profound consequences: it justified state
violence, shaped public perception, and erased the identities of thousands of victims. Using
labelling theory, this essay argues that the construction of “communist” identities during the
Jeju 4.3 Incident was not simply descriptive but actively produced a social reality that
enabled mass violence and long-term suppression of truth.
Labelling Theory: A Framework
Labelling theory, developed in sociology, suggests that deviance is not inherent in an act but
is socially constructed through labels imposed by authorities. Once individuals or groups are
labelled (e.g., “criminal” or “communist”), this label shapes how others perceive them and
how institutions treat them. Importantly, labels can become self-fulfilling and justify unequal
or violent treatment.
In the context of Jeju 4.3, the label “communist” functioned as a powerful political tool. It
transformed a diverse population—many of whom were civilians—into perceived enemies of
the state.
Historical Context and the Emergence of Labels
[[page 2]]
Following the end of Japanese colonial rule in 1945, Korea was divided along ideological
lines during the early Cold War. Jeju Island became a site of tension, with widespread
dissatisfaction toward the U.S. military government and upcoming elections. While leftist
groups were indeed active, the situation was far more complex than a simple ideological
uprising.
However, the South Korean government and allied forces framed the entire event as a
“communist insurgency.” This narrative reduced the complexity of local grievances into a
single ideological category. According to historical research, tens of thousands of people
were killed during the suppression, with government forces responsible for the majority of
civilian deaths.
This shows that the label “communist” was applied broadly, often without distinction between
armed rebels and ordinary civilians.
[[page 3]]
Institutionalisation of Labels and Everyday Control
Another critical aspect of labelling in the Jeju 4.3 Incident is how the “communist” identity
became embedded within formal institutions and everyday governance, transforming it from
a temporary political accusation into a long-term system of control. Rather than remaining a
rhetorical tool, the label was operationalised through administrative mechanisms such as
police records, residency documentation, and ideological screening processes. Under the
influence of the broader Cold War climate, South Korean authorities expanded surveillance
practices that categorised citizens based on perceived political loyalty. This meant that
labelling extended beyond those directly involved in resistance activities to include family
members, neighbours, and even entire villages. In line with labelling theory, this reflects how
deviance can be constructed and maintained through institutional reinforcement, where the
state not only defines who is “deviant” but also systematically reproduces that identity across
[[page 4]]
generations. For many Jeju residents, being associated with the label “communist” resulted
in restricted access to education, employment, and social mobility, effectively embedding
inequality into the structure of society. Schools, workplaces, and local administrations
became sites where these labels were quietly enforced, often without formal
acknowledgement but with significant consequences. Furthermore, the bureaucratisation of
labelling contributed to its persistence, as records and classifications were rarely challenged
or revised, even decades after the initial events. This created a form of “archival stigma,”
where individuals remained marked by past accusations regardless of changing political
conditions. At the same time, the normalisation of such systems led to widespread
self-censorship, as individuals adapted their behaviour to avoid suspicion. This
demonstrates how labelling operates not only through overt violence but also through subtle,
everyday practices that regulate behaviour and maintain social order. By institutionalising the
“communist” label, the state ensured that its effects would endure far beyond the immediate
period of conflict, shaping identities, opportunities, and social relations in ways that were
both pervasive and difficult to dismantle.

The Power of the “Communist” Label
[[page 5]]
The label “communist” carried extreme weight in the Cold War context. It implied disloyalty,
danger, and subversion. By applying this label to Jeju residents, the state achieved several
objectives:
1. Dehumanization
Victims were no longer seen as civilians but as enemies. This made violence against
them more acceptable. The treatment of bodies—such as disposal or
concealment—further reflects this dehumanisation and the attempt to erase
identities.
2. Legitimisation of Violence
Labelling the uprising as communist justified military suppression. It framed violence
as necessary for national security rather than as human rights violations.
3. Silencing and Fear
For decades, discussing the incident was prohibited. Victims’ families remained
silent out of fear of being associated with “communism.” The massacre was hidden
from public knowledge for nearly 50 years.
Evidence of Labelling in Media and Memory
The persistence of labellinis is evident in media representations. Until the late 1980s,
newspapers consistently described the event as a “communist riot,” reinforcing the state
narrative.
Timeline of Media Narratives
This shift demonstrates how labels are not fixed but can change over time as political
contexts evolve. However, the earlier label had already caused lasting damage by shaping
collective memory.
Social Consequences of Labelling
The labelling of Jeju residents as “communists” had long-term social consequences:
[[page 6]]
1. Stigmatisation of Survivors
Families of victims were marginalised and discriminated against. Being associated
with the incident could limit education and employment opportunities.
2. Collective Silence and Trauma
The fear of being labelled prevented open discussion, leading to what scholars
describe as intergenerational trauma. The silence itself became a continuation of
violence.
3. Distortion of Historical Truth
By controlling the narrative, the state shaped how history was understood. The
incident was not officially investigated until decades later.
Re-evaluation and the Deconstruction of Labels
With democratisation in South Korea after 1987, the dominant narrative began to change. A
truth commission established in 2000 investigated the events and recognised the suffering of
victims.
This process represents an attempt to deconstruct the label of “communist” and restore the
identities of those affected. Jeju has since been reframed as a symbol of peace and
reconciliation rather than rebellion.
However, the legacy of labelling persists. Competing interpretations—such as those
emphasising the role of communist insurgents—continue to influence political debates today.
Conclusion
The Jeju 4.3 Incident demonstrates the powerful role of labelling in shaping historical events.
Through the application of labelling theory, it becomes clear that the term “communist” was
not merely descriptive but actively constructed a social reality. It justified violence, erased
individual identities, and silenced survivors for decades.
Ultimately, the re-evaluation of the incident in recent decades shows that labels can be
challenged and redefined. However, the long-lasting effects of the original label highlight the
dangers of allowing political narratives to define human identities. The case of Jeju 4.3
serves as a reminder that labels are not neutral—they carry the power to shape lives,
histories, and collective memory.
