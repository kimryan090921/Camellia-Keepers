Intergenerational Trauma and Psychological
Repression in Survivors
Introduction
The Jeju 4.3 Incident stands as one of the most traumatic and long-silenced events in
modern Korean history. Beginning on April 3, 1948, and continuing in waves of violence until
1954, the incident resulted in the deaths of an estimated 25,000 to 30,000
people—approximately 10% of Jeju Island’s population at the time. What makes this event
particularly significant is not only the scale of violence but the enduring psychological
consequences that extended far beyond the immediate victims. Survivors were not merely
left to cope with grief; they were forced into silence by a political climate that suppressed
memory itself. As a result, the Jeju 4.3 Incident offers a powerful case study of how trauma
can be both internalized (psychological repression) and transmitted across generations
(intergenerational trauma).
Historical Context
To understand the psychological aftermath, it is necessary to first examine the historical
conditions that produced it. Following Korea’s liberation from Japanese colonial rule in 1945,
ideological tensions between left-wing and right-wing groups intensified under the influence
of Cold War geopolitics. On Jeju Island, resistance against the planned separate elections in
the South—viewed by many as cementing division—sparked protests that escalated into
armed conflict. The South Korean government, supported by U.S. military authorities,
responded with severe counterinsurgency measures. Entire villages were burned, and
civilians were indiscriminately labeled as communist sympathizers.
What followed was not only mass violence but systematic erasure. For decades, the South
Korean state framed the incident as a communist rebellion, effectively criminalizing survivors
and the bereaved. Public discussion was suppressed, and even mentioning the incident
could lead to suspicion or punishment. This prolonged silence created the conditions for
psychological repression, where individuals suppress traumatic memories not by choice, but
as a survival mechanism within an oppressive social structure.

Psychological Repression in Survivors
Psychological repression, as understood in trauma theory, refers to the unconscious
exclusion of distressing memories from conscious awareness. In the context of Jeju 4.3,
repression functioned both individually and collectively. Survivors often avoided speaking
about their experiences, even within their own families. This was not simply denial—it was a
form of protection. Speaking out could result in social ostracization, loss of employment, or
even state surveillance.
However, repression does not eliminate trauma; it merely buries it. Many survivors exhibited
symptoms now associated with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), including nightmares,
hypervigilance, and emotional numbing. Yet, due to the stigma surrounding the event and
the lack of psychological support at the time, these symptoms remained largely
unacknowledged.
One of the most striking aspects of this repression is its normalization. Silence became
embedded in daily life, shaping communication patterns and emotional expression. Parents
avoided discussing the past with their children, creating a generational gap not only in
knowledge but in emotional understanding. The absence of narrative became a defining
feature of the trauma itself.
Intergenerational Trauma
Despite this silence, the trauma of Jeju 4.3 did not end with the survivors. It was
transmitted—subtly yet powerfully—to subsequent generations. This phenomenon, known
as intergenerational trauma, occurs when the psychological effects of a traumatic event are
passed down to children and even grandchildren.
In Jeju, this transmission often occurred through indirect means: emotional atmospheres,
behavioral patterns, and fragmented stories. Children grew up sensing fear, anxiety, or
unresolved grief in their parents, even if the cause was never explicitly explained. This aligns
with psychological research suggesting that trauma can be communicated through
nonverbal cues, such as tone, silence, and emotional withdrawal.
Moreover, the social stigma attached to the incident reinforced this transmission. Families
associated with victims were sometimes labeled as “red” (communist), affecting their social
and economic opportunities. This created a persistent sense of insecurity and
marginalization that extended beyond the original event. In this way, trauma became not only
psychological but also structural, embedded in social identity and collective memory.
Interestingly, the second and third generations often experienced a form of “postmemory”—a
term coined by scholar Marianne Hirsch to describe the relationship that later generations
have with the traumatic experiences of their predecessors. These individuals inherit
memories they did not directly experience, yet feel deeply connected to them. In Jeju, many
[[page 3]]
descendants have reported a strong emotional response upon learning about the incident
later in life, often accompanied by a sense of anger, grief, or a desire for justice.
Aftermath of the survivors
The late 20th and early 21st centuries marked a turning point in the public acknowledgment
of the Jeju 4.3 Incident. Democratization in South Korea created space for historical
re-evaluation. In 2000, the government passed the Special Act on the Jeju 4.3 Incident,
leading to an official investigation and the establishment of the Jeju 4.3 Peace Foundation.
In 2003, President Roh Moo-hyun issued a formal apology on behalf of the state.
These efforts represented more than political gestures; they were crucial steps toward
psychological healing. Acknowledgment validates the experiences of survivors, allowing
them to integrate their memories into a coherent narrative rather than suppressing them.
Memorials, such as the Jeju 4.3 Peace Park, provide physical spaces where memory can be
collectively processed.
However, healing is neither immediate nor complete. For many survivors, decades of
repression have left deep psychological scars. Some have only begun to speak about their
experiences late in life, often expressing regret for the years of silence. For younger
generations, the challenge lies in reconciling inherited trauma with contemporary
identity—understanding the past without being defined by it.
Personal Reflection
What makes the Jeju 4.3 Incident particularly compelling is not just its historical significance,
but its emotional resonance in the present. Growing up in Korea—or even learning about this
event from afar—one cannot ignore the profound silence that once surrounded it. This
silence is not empty; it is filled with suppressed voices, fragmented memories, and unspoken
grief.
Reflecting on this, one realizes that trauma is not only about what is remembered, but also
about what is forgotten—or forced to be forgotten. The absence of stories can be as
powerful as their presence. In many ways, the intergenerational impact of Jeju 4.3
challenges the assumption that time alone heals wounds. Without acknowledgment and
dialogue, trauma can persist in invisible yet pervasive ways.
At the same time, there is a sense of cautious hope. The increasing openness surrounding
the incident suggests a shift from repression to recognition. Younger generations are
engaging with the past through education, art, and public discourse. This does not erase the
trauma, but it transforms it—from a source of silent suffering into a catalyst for understanding
and empathy.
Conclusion
The Jeju 4.3 Incident illustrates how trauma operates across both time and silence.
Survivors, constrained by political repression, internalized their suffering, leading to
long-term psychological consequences. Yet, this trauma did not remain confined to one
[[page 4]]
generation; it was transmitted through emotional, social, and cultural channels to their
descendants.
Understanding this process is essential not only for historical accuracy but for ethical
responsibility. Recognizing intergenerational trauma allows societies to address the lingering
effects of past violence, moving beyond denial toward meaningful reconciliation. In the case
of Jeju, the gradual breaking of silence represents a critical step in this process.
Ultimately, the legacy of Jeju 4.3 is not only one of tragedy, but also of resilience. It reminds
us that while trauma can be inherited, so too can the capacity for healing—provided that
memory is acknowledged, voices are heard, and silence is no longer enforced.
