A Landscape of Trauma: Environmental
History and the Memory of the Jeju 4·3
Incident
I. Introduction
The Jeju April Third Incident (1947–1954) was a major tragedy in modern Korean history,
resulting in the deaths and disappearance of thousands of island residents. Beyond political
history, the incident can be examined through environmental history and cultural geography.
This essay argues that Jeju’s landscapes and historical sites preserve memories of violence,
[[page 2]]
displacement, and survival, making the physical environment an essential part of historical
remembrance.
II. Landscape as Historical Evidence

Jeju’s caves, villages, and mountainous areas became closely connected with the
experiences of civilians during the incident. The 2003 government investigation report
documented widespread destruction of villages and civilian suffering. From the perspective
of environmental history, these places are more than geographical features: they help
researchers understand how violence affected everyday life and local communities. Studying
landscapes alongside oral testimonies and historical records can therefore reveal
dimensions of the past that written documents alone may overlook.
III. Memory and Difficult Heritage
Historical sites can preserve collective memory, but their meanings may remain contested.
Kim and Chung’s 2025 study of Jeju’s former detention sites uses the concept of “difficult
heritage” to explain how places associated with traumatic events are remembered and
commemorated. Their research demonstrates that preserving a site requires more than
protecting its physical structure. It also requires acknowledging the experiences of victims
and the historical circumstances surrounding the place.
IV. Historical Evidence and Public Remembrance

The documented figures demonstrate the scale of the human consequences of the incident.
However, statistics cannot fully express the loss experienced by individual families or
communities. Historical preservation, oral history, and educational visits can help connect
numerical evidence with personal experiences. Dark tourism, when conducted respectfully,
can also encourage visitors to confront difficult history rather than treating memorial sites as
ordinary attractions.
V. Conclusion
The Jeju 4·3 Incident illustrates how landscapes can become important records of historical
trauma. Environmental history explains how violence transformed communities and their
surroundings, while cultural geography and memory studies examine how these places are
remembered today. Protecting historical sites and recording survivors’ testimonies are
essential to preserving the experiences of victims. Ultimately, understanding Jeju’s
landscape as part of its historical record can strengthen public awareness and support a
more complete recognition of the past.
