From Communicative to Cultural Memory: Literature
and Art as Records of Jeju 4·3 under State Silence
8 October 2026
Abstract. For several decades after 1954, public discussion of the Jeju 4·3 Incident was suppressed in South
Korea, and memory of the events was largely confined to private recollection within families and villages.
This article examines how literary and artistic works carried that memory into the public sphere before
formal state recognition was granted through the Special Act of 2000 and the presidential apology of 2003.
Drawing on Jan Assmann's distinction between communicative and cultural memory, it analyses four
works: Hyun Ki-young's Sun-i Samchon (1978), Kim Seok-beom's Kazantō (1976–1983), Lee San-ha's
Hallasan (1987) and Kang Yo-bae's Dongbaekkkot Jida series (1989–1992). It then considers Han Kang's
We Do Not Part (2021) as an example of the later international circulation of 4·3 memory. The article argues
that these works functioned as early carriers of cultural memory at a time when the state sought to restrict it,
and that their production frequently came at considerable personal cost to their creators.
Keywords: Jeju 4·3; cultural memory; Korean literature; censorship; commemoration
1. Introduction
The Jeju 4·3 Incident refers to the armed uprising of 3 April 1948 and the counterinsurgency campaign that
followed it, which the Special Act of 2000 defines as continuing until 21 September 1954 (Ryang, 2013). Offi‐
cial estimates place the number of deaths at approximately 30,000, while figures cited within the Jeju Korean
community in Japan are considerably higher (Ryang, 2013). For decades after the conflict, the events were offi‐
cially characterised as a communist rebellion, and open discussion was restricted under anti-communist legis‐
lation (Ryang, 2013). Survivors and the families of victims had strong reasons to remain silent, since associ‐
ation with those labelled as communists carried lasting social and political consequences.
Formal acknowledgement came late. The Special Act concerning the 4·3 Incident was promulgated in
January 2000 with the stated aim of restoring the honour of victims, and President Roh Moo-hyun issued a
formal apology to the people of Jeju in October 2003 (Ryang, 2013). The subject had, however, entered pub‐
lic discourse earlier through fiction, poetry and painting. This article examines that earlier phase. It argues that
literary and artistic works acted as the first public carriers of 4·3 memory, transferring it from private recollec‐
tion into durable cultural forms, and that this process preceded, and arguably prepared the ground for, recog‐
nition by the state.

2. Theoretical Framework: Communicative and Cultural Memory
Jan Assmann distinguishes between two modes of collective memory (Assmann & Czaplicka, 1995). Commu‐
nicative memory is based on everyday interaction and oral exchange. It is informal and has a limited temporal
horizon of roughly eighty to one hundred years, or approximately three to four generations. Cultural memory,
by contrast, is sustained through objectified forms such as texts, rituals, images and monuments, which allow
memory to persist beyond the lifetimes of those who experienced the original events.
This distinction is useful for the study of 4·3. During the period of suppression, memory of the events ex‐
isted mainly in communicative form: within families, within villages and in the private observance of ancestral
rites. Without transmission into more durable forms, such memory would be expected to weaken as the gener‐
ation of survivors aged. The works discussed below can be read as attempts to make this transition under con‐
ditions in which the state actively discouraged it.
3. Literature under Censorship
3.1 Hyun Ki-young, Sun-i Samchon (1978)
Hyun Ki-young, born on Jeju in 1941, published the novella Sun-i Samchon (순이삼촌) in the literary journal
Changjak-gwa Bipyeong (Creation and Criticism) in 1978. It is widely regarded as the first literary work to ad‐
dress the 4·3 Incident directly (Literature Translation Institute of Korea [LTI Korea], n.d.). The narrator, who
lives in Seoul, learns that Aunt Suni, an elderly woman from his home village, has taken her own life. Some
thirty years earlier she had survived a massacre in which her two children were killed, and the narrator comes
to understand her death as a delayed consequence of that event (LTI Korea, n.d.). The term samchon, which
means "uncle" in standard Korean, is used in Jeju to address older neighbours regardless of sex.
The novella renames the setting as Seochon, but its account is based on the massacre at Bukchon-ri (Kim,
2019). On 17 January 1949, more than 300 residents of Bukchon-ri were killed by soldiers and police (The
Korea Times, 2019; Seoul Shinmun, 2018). One frequently cited passage describes ancestral rites beginning in
many households at the same hour, a detail that reflects the number of families who lost members on a single
day (Kim, 2019). In Assmann's terms, the passage records the point at which a village's communicative
memory, maintained through ritual, was fixed in a published text.
The consequences for the author illustrate the limits placed on such work. After publication, Hyun was
arrested and tortured, and he was released on condition that he would not write about 4·3 again (Ryang,
2013). The book was subsequently banned for a number of years (Chung, 2025). Its later commemoration in‐
dicates the change in official attitudes: in 2007, a literary monument to Sun-i Samchon was erected at Neo‐
beunsungi in Bukchon-ri, alongside a memorial hall for the victims (Seoul Shinmun, 2018).

3.2 Lee San-ha, Hallasan (1987)
The poet Lee San-ha wrote the long poem Hallasan between the autumn of 1986 and January 1987, and it
was published in March 1987 in the independent publication Nokdu Seopyeong (Weekly Kyunghyang, 2018).
In November 1987, several months after the June Democratic Struggle, Lee was arrested. He was sentenced to
imprisonment and released under a special pardon in 1988 (Weekly Kyunghyang, 2018). That the poem led to
imprisonment almost forty years after the uprising, and after the beginning of democratisation, indicates how
persistent official sensitivity towards the subject remained.
4. Memory beyond the Peninsula: Kim Seok-beom's Kazantō
Not all early representations of 4·3 were produced in South Korea. A significant number of Jeju islanders
settled in Japan, and among Koreans of Jeju origin living there, memory of the uprising was preserved and re‐
peatedly retold despite its suppression at home (Ryang, 2013). Ryang (2013) notes that these accounts placed
responsibility on mainland militias, the government of Syngman Rhee and the United States, and that they
differed from both the official South Korean and the North Korean interpretations of the events.
Kim Seok-beom, born in Osaka in 1925, wrote Kazantō (Volcano Island; Korean: Hwasando) in Japanese.
The novel was serialised in the literary monthly Bungakukai from February 1976 to August 1981 and was
published in three volumes in 1983; its narrative covers the period from approximately February to April 1948
(Ryang, 2013). Ryang chose to analyse the novel rather than to interview survivors, arguing that survivors fre‐
quently lack the language to describe what they witnessed and that fiction is able to convey this limit. She also
reads the novel's apolitical protagonist, Yi Bang-geun, as a figure for the author himself: an onlooker who was
not present at the violence (Ryang, 2013).

The case of Kazantō suggests that the cultural memory of 4·3 did not depend solely on conditions within
South Korea. Where publication at home was restricted, a diasporic community provided an alternative site in
which memory could be given textual form.
5. Visual Representation: Kang Yo-bae's Dongbaekkkot Jida
Between 1989 and 1992, the painter Kang Yo-bae produced a series of historical paintings on 4·3 based on sur‐
vivor testimony and documentary records (Kim, 2018). The series is known as Dongbaekkkot Jida (동백꽃 지
다, "The Camellia Falls"). Its title work depicts camellia blossoms falling whole from the branch, an image set
against the suffering of the people of Jeju; the same image was later used as the cover of a published collection
of 4·3 testimonies (Kim, 2009).
The camellia, which drops its entire bloom rather than shedding individual petals, has since become the
principal symbol of 4·3 commemoration and is worn as a badge at memorial events. Kang's series is significant
in this context because it gave the events a recognisable visual form. Within Assmann's framework, such im‐
ages operate as stable reference points that allow a past event to be recalled and recognised by people who did
not experience it.
6. Institutionalisation and International Reception
The period after 2000 marks a shift from unofficial to official memory. The Special Act of 2000 and the pres‐
idential apology of 2003 gave state recognition to the events (Ryang, 2013), and legal efforts have continued
since. In 2017, eighteen survivors petitioned for the reopening of convictions issued against them by military
tribunals during the Incident (Yamamoto et al., 2018). Commemoration has also continued at a local level
through the work of survivors, artists and bereaved families (Ching & Lim, 2023).
Han Kang's novel We Do Not Part (작별하지 않는다), published in 2021, represents a later stage in which
4·3 memory circulates internationally. The novel follows a writer who becomes involved in her friend's family
history, centred on the friend's mother and her lifelong search for a brother who disappeared during the Incid‐
ent (Chung, 2025). Han Kang was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in October 2024, and in 2026 the
English translation of We Do Not Part received the National Book Critics Circle Award for fiction in the
United States (Asia News Network, 2026). A subject whose literary treatment led to the arrest of authors in
1978 and 1987 has therefore entered the internationally recognised canon of Korean literature.

7. Conclusion
This article has examined the role of literature and art in preserving the memory of the Jeju 4·3 Incident dur‐
ing and after the period of state suppression. Each of the four principal works considered appeared before the
Special Act of 2000. Kazantō began serialisation twenty-four years earlier, Sun-i Samchon was published
twenty-two years earlier, Hallasan thirteen years earlier, and Kang Yo-bae's series was completed eight years
earlier. In two of these cases, the authors were arrested as a direct result of their work.
Using Assmann's distinction, these works can be understood as early mechanisms through which the
communicative memory of survivors and their families was converted into cultural memory. They made the
events available to readers and viewers with no direct connection to them, and they did so while official chan‐
nels of remembrance remained closed. The subsequent recognition of 4·3 by the state should therefore be un‐
derstood as following, rather than initiating, the public remembrance of the events.
For students and residents of Jeju today, this suggests that the literature and art of 4·3 should be studied
alongside historical and legal sources. Such works do not replace documentary evidence. They do, however,
preserve aspects of the experience that documentary records alone do not capture, particularly the long-term
effects of the violence on those who survived it.
