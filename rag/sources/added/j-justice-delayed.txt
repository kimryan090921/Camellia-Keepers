Justice Delayed: Retrials, Compensation and
Transitional Justice for Jeju 4·3, 2000–2026
15 October 2026
Abstract. More than seven decades after the Jeju 4·3 Incident, the South Korean state is still in the process
of addressing it through law. This article assesses that process using the framework set out by the United
Nations Special Rapporteur on the promotion of truth, justice, reparation and guarantees of non-
recurrence, which treats these four elements as complementary parts of a single response to mass violations.
It examines the truth-seeking work begun under the Special Act of 2000, the retrials that have overturned
the convictions issued by military tribunals in 1948 and 1949, the compensation scheme introduced by the
revised Special Act of 2021, and recent disputes over commemoration and official honours. The article finds
that the period since 2021 marks a shift from symbolic recognition to enforceable legal remedies. By July
2025, 2,033 people had been acquitted through retrials initiated by the state, and by July 2026,
compensation of 723.9 billion won had been paid in respect of 9,344 victims. It also identifies three
limitations: the long delay, which means that most remedies now reach heirs rather than victims; the absence
of individual accountability for those responsible for the violence; and the continuing contestation of the
official account.
Keywords: Jeju 4·3; transitional justice; retrial; reparations; Special Act
1. Introduction
Between 1948 and 1954, the uprising on Jeju and the counterinsurgency campaign that followed resulted in
an estimated 30,000 deaths, according to official figures (Ryang, 2013). For roughly fifty years, demands for
truth and justice from victims and their families were suppressed under successive authoritarian governments
(Kim, 2009). The process of addressing these events through state institutions began only in 2000, when the
Special Act on the 4·3 Incident created what Kim (2009) describes as South Korea's first truth commission.
Since then, the response has expanded considerably. A government investigation report was published in
2003 and was followed by a presidential apology in October of that year (Ryang, 2013). In 2014, 3 April was
designated a national memorial day (Seoul Shinmun, 2014). From 2019, the courts began to overturn convic‐
tions issued by military tribunals during the Incident, and in 2021 the National Assembly passed a revised
Special Act that provided for compensation and for retrials initiated by the state (Son, 2021). By 2026, the
state had recognised 15,286 people as victims and 128,295 as bereaved family members (Jeong, 2026).
This article asks how far these measures amount to a complete response. It uses the framework of trans‐
itional justice developed by the UN Special Rapporteur Pablo de Greiff (2012), and it argues that the revised
Act of 2021 is the most important turning point in the process, because it changed the position of victims
from people to be commemorated into holders of legal rights. It also argues that the delay in delivering these
remedies has limited what they can achieve.

2. Theoretical Framework: Four Elements of Transitional Justice
De Greiff (2012) identifies four measures through which states respond to gross violations of human rights:
truth-seeking, justice initiatives, reparations, and guarantees of non-recurrence. His central claim is that these
measures are complementary rather than interchangeable. Truth-seeking without justice does not amount to
justice, and reparations without truth-telling may be seen as no more than a payment. For this reason, he
warns that states should not trade one measure against another (de Greiff, 2012, paras. 22–27).
De Greiff also sets out the goals that these measures are intended to serve. Their mediate goals are recogni‐
tion, meaning that victims are acknowledged as holders of rights and not only as people who suffered, and
trust, both between citizens and in state institutions. Their final goals are reconciliation and the strengthening
of the rule of law (de Greiff, 2012, paras. 28–46). This framework is useful for the Jeju case because measures
for 4·3 have been introduced separately over more than two decades, and it allows each to be assessed in rela‐
tion to the others. Figure 1 groups the main measures under the four elements.

3. Truth-Seeking
The Special Act of 2000 established a committee to investigate the Incident and to determine who should be
recognised as victims. Kim (2009) notes that the commission was the product of a grassroots campaign by stu‐
dents, activists and journalists that began after democratisation in 1987. The government report published in
2003 became the official account of the Incident and remains the main reference point for later legal decisions.
Truth-seeking has not ended with the report. The recognition of victims and bereaved families still de‐
pends on applications from families, and the eighth round of additional registration in 2023 received 19,559
applications; a ninth round is planned for 2027 (Jeong, 2026). In April 2025, the archive "Revealing Truth:
Jeju 4·3 Archives" was inscribed on the UNESCO Memory of the World Register. It contains 14,673 items,

4. Justice: Overturning the Military Tribunals
4.1 The convictions of 1948–1949
During the Incident, 2,530 people were imprisoned following trials by military tribunals and received sen‐
tences of between one and twenty years on charges including insurrection (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 2019). The
trials did not follow proper procedure: there were no written indictments or trial records (Kyunghyang Shin‐
mun, 2019). Those convicted were sent to prisons across the country, and most were killed and buried in un‐
marked graves after the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, which is why many are recorded as missing (Lee
& Hwang, 2021).
4.2 Retrials, 2019–2025
In 2017, eighteen survivors of these trials petitioned for their cases to be reopened (Yamamoto et al., 2018).
On 17 January 2019, the Jeju District Court dismissed the charges against them, holding that trials which did
not specify charges or follow proper procedure were invalid (Kyunghyang Shinmun, 2019). On 16 March
2021, the same court acquitted 335 people, 333 of whom had been missing since the war; the prosecution it‐
self asked for acquittal on the grounds that there was no evidence for the original charges (Lee & Hwang,
2021).
The revised Special Act, passed on 26 February 2021, made this process systematic. It provided for retrials
of military tribunal convictions to be initiated by the state, without requiring each family to apply, and for
special retrials of convictions issued by ordinary courts (Son, 2021). A joint task force for state-initiated retrials
began work on 10 February 2022, and in August 2022 the Ministry of Justice announced that these retrials
would be extended to people convicted by ordinary courts, a group estimated at more than 1,500 people
(Shin, 2022). By July 2025, retrials had been requested for 2,171 people, and 2,033 had been acquitted: 1,711
convicted by military tribunals and 322 convicted by ordinary courts (Lim, 2025). As Figure 2(a) shows, this
represents about two-thirds of those imprisoned by the military tribunals.

These retrials represent a form of justice centred on victims: they correct the criminal records of those who
were wrongly convicted. They do not, however, address the responsibility of those who carried out the
killings. Wolman (2013) notes that critics of Korean truth commissions, including the Jeju commission, re‐
gard the failure to identify perpetrators as a serious weakness. In de Greiff's terms, the justice element in the
Jeju case has been limited to one of its possible forms.
5. Reparation
For two decades after the Special Act, reparation for 4·3 was largely symbolic, taking the form of the presiden‐
tial apology and state memorial services. The revised Act of 2021 changed this by providing for payments of a
consolatory nature to victims and their families. The bill passed with 199 votes in favour, 5 against and 25 ab‐
stentions, and the government estimated the cost at around 1.3 trillion won (Son, 2021). A further amend‐
ment in December 2021 set the amount at up to 90 million won per victim (Lim, 2021).
The first round of payments was approved in October 2022. Of 300 victims approved, 220 who had died
or gone missing received 90 million won each, 77 survivors with lasting injuries received between 50 and 90
million won, and three people who had been wrongly imprisoned received between 30 and 90 million won
(The Korea Herald, 2022). Table 1 shows the position by July 2026.

One figure stands out. Payments for 9,344 victims were made to 98,805 claimants, an average of more
than ten heirs for each victim (Jin, 2026). This reflects the length of the delay. The scheme compensates the
families of victims who, in most cases, died decades ago, and the money is divided among their descendants.
Reparation has therefore arrived as recognition of a family's loss more than as support for the individuals who
suffered it.
6. Non-Recurrence and the Contested Record
Guarantees of non-recurrence are weakened if the official account of past violations is not widely accepted. In
the case of 4·3, that acceptance remains incomplete. When the revised Act was passed in 2021, opponents ar‐
gued that it was unconstitutional, that responsibility lay with the Workers' Party of South Korea, and that per‐
petrators might be recognised as victims (Son, 2021). In February 2023, a lawmaker of the governing party,
Tae Yong-ho, claimed that the uprising had been started on the orders of Kim Il-sung (Nam, 2023). On 10
December 2025, the Jeju District Court found that these statements were false when measured against the
government's investigation report, and ordered him to pay 10 million won to the Jeju 4·3 Victims' Bereaved
Families Association (Lee, 2025).
A second case shows how state institutions themselves can contradict the official account. Colonel Park
Jin-gyeong commanded the 9th Regiment on Jeju from May 1948, and according to the government report he
arrested around 3,000 islanders in about forty days before he was assassinated by his own subordinates in June
1948 (Kang, 2026). On 4 November 2025, his registration as a person of national merit was approved on the
basis of a military decoration awarded in 1950. Following objections from Jeju, the President ordered a review
on 14 December 2025, and on 26 February 2026 the registration was effectively cancelled on the grounds that
it had not been considered by the review committee required by law (Kang, 2026).
Both cases ended in favour of the official account. They nevertheless show that, more than twenty years
after the 2003 report, that account still has to be defended in court and through administrative review. In de
Greiff's terms, the trust that transitional justice aims to build has not yet been fully secured.
7. Discussion
Read against de Greiff's framework, the Jeju case supports his argument that the four elements depend on
each other. The 2003 report was the basis on which the courts found no evidence for the original convictions,
and it was also the standard against which a court judged the statements of a sitting lawmaker to be false.
Without the truth-seeking phase, neither the retrials nor the defamation ruling would have had a factual
foundation.
The case also shows the significance of the revised Act of 2021. Before 2021, victims were recognised
mainly through apology and commemoration. Since 2021, they and their families have been able to claim spe‐
cific legal remedies: the reversal of a conviction and a defined sum of compensation. This corresponds to de
Greiff's distinction between recognising victims as people who suffered and recognising them as holders of
rights.

Three limitations remain. First, the delay of more than seventy years means that most remedies reach heirs
rather than victims, as the ratio of claimants to victims in Table 1 shows. Second, justice has taken the form of
acquitting victims rather than establishing the responsibility of perpetrators (Wolman, 2013). Third, the offi‐
cial account continues to be challenged, and its defence has depended on individual court cases and adminis‐
trative reversals rather than settled political consensus.
8. Conclusion
This article has assessed the response to the Jeju 4·3 Incident between 2000 and 2026 using the four elements
of transitional justice. Truth-seeking began in 2000 and is still continuing through new applications for recog‐
nition. Justice has taken the form of retrials, which by July 2025 had acquitted 2,033 people. Reparation, sym‐
bolic for twenty years, became financial after 2021, with 723.9 billion won paid by July 2026. Guarantees of
non-recurrence remain the least secure element, as recent disputes over a politician's statements and a military
officer's honours show.
The revised Special Act of 2021 was the turning point, because it turned recognition into enforceable
rights. The main lesson of the Jeju case, however, concerns time. The remedies now available are substantial,
but they have arrived after almost all of those directly affected had died. For students and residents of Jeju, this
suggests that remembrance of 4·3 should include attention to the legal process, which is not yet complete, as
well as to the events of 1948 to 1954 themselves.
