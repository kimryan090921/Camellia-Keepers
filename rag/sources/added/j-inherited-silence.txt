Inherited Silence: Intergenerational Trauma and
Collective Memory in the Legacy of the Jeju 4.3
Incident
Mass violence does not end when the killing stops. Psychologists and sociologists
increasingly argue that its deepest effects are carried in memory, family relationships, and
the unspoken rules of a community. Collective trauma, as Kai Erikson (1976) described it, is
damage to the social tissue itself: the bonds of trust and shared life that make a community a
community. Intergenerational trauma extends this idea across time, suggesting that people
who never witnessed the violence can still inherit its emotional weight.
The Jeju 4.3 Incident is a striking case for this field. Between 1948 and 1954, state
suppression on Jeju Island killed an estimated 25,000 to 30,000 people, roughly one tenth of
the island's population (Jeju 4·3 Peace Foundation, n.d.). What distinguishes Jeju is the
decades of enforced silence that followed. Survivors were forbidden from mourning publicly,
which meant that grief went unprocessed for two generations.
I. Collective Trauma and the Politics of Silence
Trauma research holds that recovery depends on acknowledgment: the survivor's suffering
must be recognized as real and as wrong. After 1948, Jeju residents were denied exactly
this. The state labeled the island politically suspect, and under the system of guilt by
association (yeon-jwa-je), relatives of victims faced surveillance and exclusion from public
employment (Kim, 2014). Speaking about the dead could endanger the living.
The result was what scholars call a culture of silence. Families held memorials in secret,
often on the same night for many households, and parents avoided telling children what had
happened to their relatives. Jeffrey Alexander and colleagues (2004) argue that trauma
becomes "cultural" only when a society can narrate it publicly. On Jeju, that narration was
blocked, so the wound remained private, unresolved, and unspoken.
II. The Transmission of Trauma Across Generations
If trauma is never discussed, it does not disappear. Research on children of Holocaust
survivors, for example, shows that parental trauma can shape children's anxiety, attachment,
and sense of safety, even when the story itself is withheld. Yehuda and Lehrner (2018)
review evidence that these effects may involve both psychosocial pathways, such as
parenting shaped by fear, and possible epigenetic mechanisms, though they stress that the
biological findings remain preliminary and contested.
The psychosocial pathway is the most relevant to Jeju. Parents who had survived the
massacres taught their children to stay quiet, avoid politics, and never draw attention to
themselves. This protective instinct made sense under surveillance, but it also passed fear
down the generations. Children grew up sensing a loss they could not name, a pattern the

III. Postmemory and the Role of Culture
Marianne Hirsch (2012) coined the term "postmemory" for the relationship that later
generations have with traumatic events they did not experience but know through stories,
images, and silences. For Jeju's younger generations, postmemory formed through
fragments: a missing grandfather, a burned village site, an anniversary no one explained.
[[page 3]]
Culture became an important space where these fragments could be spoken. Writers and
artists on Jeju worked to preserve the history long before the state officially acknowledged it,
and the island's literary tradition kept the memory alive when public discussion was
impossible. This tradition reached a global audience when Han Kang, whose novel We Do
Not Part (2021) engages with the 4.3 Incident, received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2024.
Literature allowed a wound that could not be discussed politically to be approached through
imagination and empathy.
IV. Truth-Seeking as Psychological Repair
Democratization created the conditions for healing. Hun Joon Kim (2014) traces how
decades of activism by survivors, bereaved families, and civil society eventually produced
the 2000 Special Act on the Jeju 4·3 Incident and the 2003 state apology by President Roh
Moo-hyun. From a psychological perspective, these were not only political events. Official
acknowledgment gave survivors the public validation that trauma research identifies as
essential to recovery: the state was finally saying that what happened to them was real and
wrong.
Later measures built on this foundation. Retrials beginning in 2020 acquitted survivors who
had been wrongly convicted by military courts, and revisions to the Special Act opened a
path to compensation. Institutions such as the Jeju 4·3 Peace Park and the island's
trauma-healing programs now give grief a public place. Memorials, in this sense, work as
shared mental health infrastructure, allowing mourning that was once forced into private
spaces to become collective.
Conclusion
Seen through the lens of psychology and memory studies, the Jeju 4.3 Incident shows that
violence leaves two kinds of casualties: those who died, and those who were prevented from
grieving them. The decades of enforced silence turned a single historical event into a
multigenerational wound, carried through fear, stigma, and absent stories.
The island's gradual recovery suggests that healing requires more than the passage of time.
It requires truth, recognition, and spaces where memory can be shared safely. For societies
confronting their own histories of mass violence, Jeju offers a lesson that is as psychological
as it is political: what a community is not allowed to remember, its children may be left to
carry.
